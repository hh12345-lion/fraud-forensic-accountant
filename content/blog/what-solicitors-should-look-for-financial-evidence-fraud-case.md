Financial evidence can form an important part of a fraud case. Bank statements, accounting records, invoices, transaction data, contracts and other financial documents may need to be reviewed to understand the transactions relevant to the issues being considered.

For solicitors, reviewing financial evidence is not simply a matter of finding unusual payments or discrepancies. The records need to be considered in context, with attention to their source, completeness, consistency and relevance to the questions in the case.

A **fraud forensic accountant** may assist with the financial analysis where a matter requires specialist accounting expertise. The scope of that work will depend on the instructions, available records and issues being examined.

This article outlines some of the areas solicitors may consider when reviewing financial evidence in a fraud case.

## Establishing What Financial Records Are Available
The first consideration may be the overall set of financial records available for review.

Depending on the circumstances, these could include:

- Bank statements
- General ledgers
- Trial balances
- Invoices and receipts
- Payment records
- Payroll records
- Credit card statements
- Loan documents
- Accounting system data
- Company financial records
- Contracts
- Tax-related records
- Records relating to asset ownership

It may also be useful to identify which individuals, companies, accounts and periods are represented in the records.

A clear understanding of the available evidence can help identify areas where additional financial information may be relevant.

## Checking the Scope and Date Range
Financial evidence needs to be considered against the period relevant to the matter.

Solicitors may wish to establish:

- When the relevant financial activity is alleged to have occurred
- Which accounts were active during the relevant period
- Whether the records cover the entire period being examined
- Whether there are gaps in the available statements
- Whether records from before or after the relevant period provide useful context

The appropriate period will depend on the facts and issues in the particular case.

A gap in the records does not, by itself, establish why information is unavailable or what the missing information would show. It may simply identify an area requiring further investigation or clarification.

## Considering the Source of the Financial Evidence
The source of a document can affect how it should be understood and compared with other records.

For example, a bank statement may provide information about transactions passing through an account, while an accounting ledger may record how those transactions were classified by a business.

Similarly, an invoice may provide information about an apparent transaction, while a bank record may provide evidence about whether a corresponding payment occurred.

Considering the source of each document can help avoid treating different types of financial evidence as though they provide the same information.

## Checking Whether Records Are Consistent
Financial records may contain information that can be compared across different sources.

For example, solicitors may consider whether:

- Bank transactions correspond with accounting entries
- Payments correspond with invoices
- Loan records correspond with relevant transfers
- Financial statements are consistent with underlying records
- Transaction dates are consistent across documents
- Amounts recorded in different documents correspond

Differences do not necessarily indicate fraud or wrongdoing. They may arise from timing differences, accounting treatment, incomplete records or other circumstances.

The significance of a discrepancy therefore needs to be considered alongside the available evidence.

## Looking at Transactions in Context
An individual transaction may appear unusual without the surrounding information needed to understand it.

For example, a payment to another company may require consideration of:

- The relationship between the parties
- The reason for the payment
- Any underlying agreement
- Related invoices
- The accounting treatment
- Subsequent transactions
- Other relevant financial records

The presence of an unusual transaction does not necessarily establish improper conduct.

A **forensic accounting** review may help place relevant transactions within the wider financial information available.

## Examining Transfers Between Accounts
Transfers between accounts can be particularly important where funds move through multiple accounts or entities.

A review may consider:

1. The originating account
2. The date and value of the transfer
3. The receiving account
4. Any subsequent transfers
5. Supporting documentation
6. The relationship between the relevant parties

This type of analysis may help establish how funds moved through the accounts.

However, tracing a transaction does not necessarily establish its purpose or legal significance. Those questions depend on the wider evidence and the scope of the investigation.

## Identifying Missing Supporting Documents
A transaction may appear in a bank statement or ledger without all of the supporting documentation being available.

Depending on the circumstances, relevant supporting records might include:

- Invoices
- Contracts
- Receipts
- Purchase orders
- Loan agreements
- Payroll records
- Correspondence
- Accounting entries

The absence of supporting documentation may be relevant to the financial review, but it should not automatically be treated as evidence that the underlying transaction was improper.

Where appropriate, the absence or limitation can be documented for further consideration.

## Reviewing Transaction Patterns
Large financial datasets can contain repeated transactions, transfers or payments that may be relevant to the issues being investigated.

Solicitors may consider whether the records show:

- Repeated payments to the same recipient
- Transfers between related accounts
- Changes in transaction values
- Changes in payment frequency
- Transactions involving related parties
- Payments that require further explanation

These patterns can provide areas for closer examination.

They should, however, be interpreted in the context of the underlying financial records rather than treated as conclusive evidence in isolation.

## Considering Related Parties and Entities
Where several companies, directors, shareholders or other individuals are involved, it may be relevant to understand the financial relationships between them.

The review may include:

- Payments between related entities
- Intercompany loans
- Shared expenses
- Transfers between company and personal accounts
- Common financial arrangements
- Other transactions involving connected parties

A financial relationship between parties does not necessarily indicate wrongdoing. Its relevance will depend on the facts and the questions being examined.

## Checking the Reliability of Financial Data
Large volumes of electronic financial data may require additional consideration before conclusions are drawn from the analysis.

Depending on the source, issues may include:

- Duplicate records
- Missing entries
- Inconsistent transaction descriptions
- Different date formats
- Changes in accounting classifications
- Data extracted from different systems

Where data has been processed or converted for analysis, it may also be appropriate to understand how that process was carried out.

This can help ensure that financial analysis remains connected to the underlying source records.

## Distinguishing Financial Evidence From Interpretation
An important consideration is the distinction between what the records directly show and what may be inferred from them.

For example, a bank statement may show that a payment was made from one account to another. It may not, by itself, establish why the payment was made or what the parties intended.

Similarly, an accounting entry may show how a transaction was recorded but may not provide the complete explanation for the underlying transaction.

Keeping the factual financial information separate from interpretation can help make the analysis clearer.

## Considering Whether Specialist Financial Analysis Is Required
Some fraud cases involve financial issues that may be relatively straightforward. Others can involve extensive transaction records, multiple accounts, related entities or complex financial arrangements.

Where the financial issues are sufficiently complex, a solicitor may consider whether specialist **fraud forensic accounting** expertise would assist with the analysis.

Depending on the engagement, a forensic accountant may be asked to:

- Review financial records
- Analyse transactions
- Reconcile different datasets
- Trace relevant financial activity
- Identify inconsistencies
- Prepare financial schedules
- Explain accounting issues within the scope of the instruction

The precise work will depend on the instructions and whether the accountant is being asked to provide investigative assistance, accounting analysis or independent expert evidence.

## Understanding the Limits of the Available Evidence
Financial analysis is necessarily affected by the quality and completeness of the records available.

Limitations may arise where:

- Bank statements are incomplete
- Accounting records are unavailable
- Transactions cannot be fully traced
- Supporting documents are missing
- Records cover different periods
- Information from different sources conflicts

These limitations should be identified rather than overlooked.

A careful review can distinguish between information supported by the available records and matters that remain uncertain or require further evidence.

## Preparing Financial Evidence for Further Review
Where financial evidence is likely to form part of legal proceedings, it may be useful to organise relevant records in a way that allows the underlying transactions and supporting documents to be identified clearly.

Depending on the matter, this could include:

- Chronological transaction schedules
- Reconciliations
- Account summaries
- Transaction listings
- Supporting document references
- Explanations of identified discrepancies

The appropriate format will depend on the purpose of the analysis and the requirements of the particular matter.

Where independent expert evidence is required, the scope and presentation of that evidence should reflect the applicable requirements and the accountant's instructions.

## Questions Solicitors May Consider
When reviewing financial evidence in a fraud case, useful questions may include:

- What financial records are available?
- What periods do the records cover?
- Which accounts and entities are represented?
- Are there gaps in the records?
- Do bank records correspond with accounting records?
- Are relevant transactions supported by documentation?
- Can particular payments or transfers be traced?
- Are there discrepancies requiring further examination?
- Are related parties or entities involved?
- Does the financial evidence address the specific questions arising in the case?
- Would specialist forensic accounting analysis assist with the financial issues?

These questions can help keep the review focused on the evidence and issues relevant to the matter.

## Conclusion
Reviewing financial evidence in a fraud case requires more than identifying transactions that appear unusual. The source, date range, completeness, consistency and context of the records may all be relevant to understanding their significance.

Solicitors may also need to distinguish between what financial records demonstrate directly and what may require additional evidence or specialist analysis. Where records are extensive or financially complex, a **fraud forensic accountant** might be instructed to analyse the available information within a clearly defined scope.

A structured approach can help identify gaps, inconsistencies and transactions requiring further examination while maintaining an appropriate distinction between financial evidence and conclusions drawn from that evidence.
